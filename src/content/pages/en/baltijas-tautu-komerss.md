title: Baltic Nations’ Commers

Commerses of the nations, that is, festive gatherings held to strengthen friendship and to mark an anniversary, go back to the days of tsarist Russia and of independence. Yet they gained real weight and became regular only after the Second World War, when the Baltic states had been occupied by the USSR and hundreds of thousands of refugees from Latvia and Estonia were living in exile in the West. A very large share of them belonged to the best-educated part of society, and so many members of fraternities, too, found themselves in the free world.

By the early 1960s some of the Baltic German fraternity members had resumed their activities. In 1939 most of them had left their native Latvia or Estonia for their “historical homeland”, Germany. Relations between Estonians and Latvians on the one side and the local Germans on the other had never been simple. After the Second World War it turned out that the Latvians and Estonians in exile and the Baltic Germans alike had lost their homeland, and with it a part of their identity. To make up for this in some measure, and to keep up their ties with fellow countrymen who had ended up in exile, the philisters of the Baltic Germans proposed in the 1960s the tradition of a regular Baltic Nations’ Commers.

At first the commerses were held in Heidelberg, the ancient stronghold of students. Although the organising was done by Baltic German philisters, the commers was led by one Baltic German, one Estonian and one Latvian, which underlined the classic values of fraternity life: solidarity, equality and respect. In the early 1990s Latvia and Estonia regained their independence, almost all the fraternities quickly resumed their work in both countries, and a few years later, in 1994, the arrangement of the Baltic Nations’ Commers was changed. The Estonian, Latvian and Baltic German convents took it in turn to organise the commers. The fraternity whose turn it was had to take on all the organising and to run every part of the event: the ball, the church service, the procession, the Landesvater ceremony and the Kater on the day after. From the 1990s the work was made somewhat easier by the sororities joining in the organising in Latvia and Estonia. The number of participants in each commers usually runs into the hundreds. Young and old alike come to these festivities to affirm their friendship. They spend warm hours in the company of people of different nations, enjoying the fact that the same values unite us all. For guests from abroad it is also an excellent chance to get to know another country with the help of old and newly won friends. The first to take the baton of organising the Baltic Nations’ Commers from the Germans was the Estonian fraternity Vironia, in Tartu. A year later came the commers held by Fraternitas Metropolitana in Riga (one of the rare occasions on which the Landesvater followed the custom of Tartu), and in 1996 Concordia Rigensis organised the commers in Hamburg. Then came Ugala in Tartu in 1997, Lacuania in Riga in 1998, Curonia Goettingensis in Göttingen in 1999, and in 2000 the commers was held in Tallinn for the first time, organised by Leola. In 2001 Selonija led a well-attended Baltic Nations’ Commers in Riga, followed by Fraternitas Dorpatensis in Munich in 2002, Fraternitas Tartuensis in Tartu in 2003, Tervetia in Riga in 2004, Concordia Rigensis in Hamburg in 2005, Sakala in Tartu in 2006, Patria in Riga in 2007, Curonia Goettingensis in Göttingen in 2008 and finally, in 2009, a splendid commers held by Fraternitas Liviensis. In Germany the Baltic Nations’ Commers is organised by the fraternities in turn, while in Estonia and Latvia it falls to the fraternities that preside in the semester in question. It should be noted that from 2011 a fourth country, Poland, will take part in organising the Baltic Nations’ Commers as an equal partner. In 2007 it was decided in Riga that the three Polish fraternities that once belonged to the Baltic, now in Warsaw and Gdańsk, are also entitled to the honour of leading these great gatherings of Baltic fraternity members. Fraternitas Cursica, the organiser of the Baltic Nations’ Commers of 2010, is the youngest fraternity ever to have held an international event on this scale. Its members will do everything they can so that those who take part remember the events of that weekend in June this year with the best of memories.

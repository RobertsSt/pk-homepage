---
title: What is a student fraternity?
description: A student fraternity is a lifelong academic organisation of students and graduates, built on brotherhood, seniority, a code of honour and ritual.
translatedFrom: 2024-02-04T10:08:07
---

A student fraternity is a lifelong academic organisation made up of students and of those who have completed their studies, the philisters. The fraternity did not come about in a short time; it took shape, and arrived at its present form of fellowship, over several centuries. A fraternity is not a student society held together only by a certain way of life. It carries a definite ideology and firm principles that may not be broken.

Dr. iur. V. Sinaiskis of Fraternitas Arctica sees four basic principles at the foundation of a fraternity:

**The idea of brotherhood**, whose aim is mutual understanding and growing together towards a common ideal. The terms used among fraternity members, fraternitas (brotherhood) and commilito (comrade in arms), show how old this idea is.

**The principle of seniority**, which rests on respect and friendship between the older and the younger and binds both in the discipline and traditions of a family. Seniority in a fraternity is set by how long a member has belonged to the organisation, not by his age. The younger always defers to the older, on the basis of mutual respect. This holds in internal and in external life alike. In internal life the younger defers to the older both by the number of semesters and by the rights of office. Among the members of a fraternity this principle has no exceptions. These are rights that run through the structure of the fraternity from the oldest semester to the youngest and carry on from semester to semester. The only exception is matters of honour. There the principle of seniority has no bearing, and in settling them all members of the fraternity have equal rights, whatever their number of semesters.

**The code of honour**, which in its historical sense means that a society which keeps such a code settles matters of honour among its members as among equals. Students have formed such societies ever since they gained academic freedom. History shows that at certain times these rights were guaranteed to students by the ruler, by the power of the state. The fraternities have kept this principle and hold the idea of honour in high regard. Any member of the society who endangers this principle or fails to observe it is punished by the other members within the laws of the society. The comment of honour binds all fraternity members.

**Ritus (order)**. The aim of this principle is to order the conduct of the fraternities and their members at certain internal occasions. The rituals have clear ideal purposes, and each has its meaning. The Landesvater (Zemes tēvs), for example, is the fraternity members’ profession of faith in their principles, their nation, their fatherland and their fraternity.

All four principles are equally important and are bound up with one another.

For a fraternity to function, that is, to bring its principles to life in the forms of its social life, the society makes high demands of its members. Meeting these demands is at times not only hard; it also takes much moral strength, self-formation and self-control.

At the root of a fraternity’s work is honour, a sense of honour that the fraternities have cultivated and instil in their members. Without this quality a fraternity cannot exist. In the fraternities, which grew out of the medieval “nations”, national consciousness and self-respect hold an important place. The sense of honour is closely tied to love of the fatherland and to the idea of freedom. Only in a society where the rights of every member rest on democratic principles can constructive ideas arise and decisions be taken that benefit each member and the society as a whole. In such a society real order, upheld by all its members, can prevail.

The structure and working of a fraternity are a model of true democracy, and it does not restrict the individuality or the freedom of spirit of its members. A fraternity is a disciplined organisation whose activity is governed by comments (laws) and usus (unwritten laws). Nothing in the life of a fraternity happens by chance, for it runs within the comments and is determined by the decisions of the convent. Discipline forms a conscientious person who is used to controlling his conduct; it builds character and bearing. The discipline of the convent requires that the tasks set by the society be carried out first, and their necessity questioned only afterwards.

The traditions of the fraternities lay down that the life of a fraternity is strictly internal. It does not show outwardly, and what happens there is not spoken of in society at large.

A fraternity is an organisation for life. Those who belong to it remain loyal to one another to the grave. For that reason the fraternities do not allow a member to share this loyalty with another fraternity, that is, to belong to more than one. A fraternity is an organisation for life in a deeper sense as well. It may interrupt its activity, or even end it, but its members cannot close it, because the comments make no provision for that. When active life has ended, a fraternity becomes a society of traditions, whose existence ends when its last member passes away. So as not to divide the society and to keep it of one mind, political and religious questions are not debated in the convent. Membership of an organisation of another kind, of a political party or of a religious group is a member’s private affair. A fraternity member may not, however, belong to organisations and groups whose aims run counter to the principles and aims of the fraternities. A fraternity is an organisation with a firmly stated national and anti-Marxist stance.

Members are chosen by a principle of selection. One cannot simply join; new members are admitted with the consent of the majority of the society. So that the spirit of the fraternity and friendship can be better instilled in the newly admitted, new members are taken in small numbers. Such small groups of the newly admitted form what are called coetus. The coetus in turn make up the fraternity as families make up a state. After admission the newcomer is an equal member of the society, who has to acquire the upbringing of a fraternity member in the way the laws of the fraternities prescribe.

The fraternities instil in their members a deep sense of duty and the courage to defend their nation, their state and society, every member of their fraternity, themselves and the truth, if need be with weapon in hand.

Following the ideals of the fraternities in personal life and in the life of the organisation is what forms the fraternity spirit. It has grown up together with students and the university and will never lose the values it has gathered over the centuries. Nor will a person who follows these principles lose his worth.
